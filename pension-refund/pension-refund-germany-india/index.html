Pension refund from Germany for Indian citizens

If you worked in Germany, paid into the state pension and have left, you may be able to get back the part of the contributions you paid yourself. This page explains the rules for Indian citizens in general terms. It is not advice about your case. The German pension insurance (Deutsche Rentenversicherung) decides every application.

The short version. For an Indian citizen, three things must hold:

  1. You have fewer than 60 months with contributions.
  2. You do not live in the EU.
  3. 24 full calendar months have passed since your last contribution month.

Then read the section on the one question this page cannot settle. It could change the answer for you.

Who qualifies, in general terms

The pension fund's English brochure for India says you may apply for a refund if your insurance liability in Germany has ended, you cannot insure yourself voluntarily, and at least 24 months have passed since the liability ended (brochure, PDF page 11).

The brochure then says when an Indian citizen may insure voluntarily (PDF page 9):

  • If you live in India, you may pay voluntary contributions once you have paid 60 months. The same applies in other countries outside the EU, such as Mexico or Russia.
  • If you live in the EU, you may pay voluntary contributions after a single contribution.

Where voluntary insurance is open, no refund is paid. That gives the working rule:

Your situation Refund route in general terms
Under 60 months, living in India or elsewhere outside the EU Open once the 24 months are over
60 months or more Closed. You may be entitled to a pension at retirement age instead
Living in an EU country Closed while you live there

Guides and agencies state the rule more directly. All About Berlin lists "less than 60 monthly pension payments", a last payment more than 24 months ago, and not living in the EU or the UK (All About Berlin guide). Germany Pension Refund lists India among the countries with a 60-month limit and says a part month counts as a full month (Germany Pension Refund).

The UK. The guides above treat the UK like the EU. The brochure names only the EU. Ask the pension fund if this is your case.

The 24-month wait

The law says a refund is paid only when 24 calendar months have passed since you left compulsory insurance, and compulsory insurance has not started again (§ 210 (2) SGB VI). In practice the first day you can apply is the first day of the 25th month after your last contribution month (Germany Pension Refund).

There is a full explanation, with examples, in When can I claim my German pension refund?

What you get back

You get back only the part of the contributions you paid yourself. The brochure says that employees receive "only that part of their compulsory contributions that they paid themselves", with no interest (brochure, PDF page 12). Your employer's half is not refunded.

The pension insurance rate is 18.6%, so your share is 9.3%. The monthly ceiling from 1 January 2026 is €8,450. Pay above the ceiling is not charged (Deutsche Rentenversicherung, "Werte der Rentenversicherung").

A rough example: two years at €40,000 a year is 24 months, so 9.3% of €80,000 is €7,440. All About Berlin's calculator gives the same figure for those inputs (calculator). The real figure depends on your actual pay, the rates and ceilings of each year, and your insurance record. The pension fund works it out. The free date checker gives a rough estimate and shows its formula.

When a refund is not paid

The brochure lists two cases (PDF page 12):

  • You already receive a foreign pension that can only be paid because German and foreign contributions were added together.
  • You already received benefits based on those contributions, for example medical rehabilitation. Contributions paid after such benefits may still be refunded.

The one question this page cannot settle

The brochure also says:

"Insurance liability in a Member state of the European Union or in a country with which Germany has concluded a social security agreement can be regarded as equivalent to the liability to be insured in Germany. This means that contributions cannot be refunded. Please seek advice, if applicable." (brochure, PDF page 11)

India has such an agreement with Germany. It took effect on 1 May 2017 (brochure, PDF page 4). The brochure explains that India's Employees' Provident Fund is the statutory pension insurance there (PDF page 32).

The brochure does not say whether insurance in India, for example in a provident fund after your return, counts as "equivalent". We could not find a clear answer either. So this page does not say you are blocked, and it does not say you are free. If you pay into a provident fund in India, ask the pension fund before you apply.

Before you apply: a refund ends your German insurance

The law says a refund ends the insurance relationship, and rights from the earlier months no longer exist (§ 210 (6) SGB VI). The brochure advises: "Please seek advice before you apply for a refund of your contributions. A pension may be the more favourable alternative." (PDF page 12)

Documents

The pension fund publishes the refund forms in one package. For an application from abroad the form is V0901, in German and English. The fund's page says it must be sent with officially confirmed proof of your nationality, and that the form contains the payment declaration needed for a transfer abroad (Deutsche Rentenversicherung, form V0901; form package).

All About Berlin's guide lists what people usually send (guide):

  • your tax ID and social insurance number
  • a copy of your passport, certified by a German embassy or consulate
  • a copy of your registration certificate for social insurance (Meldebescheinigung zur Sozialversicherung); an older one is accepted
  • your deregistration certificate (Abmeldebescheinigung), which is not always asked for
  • proof that you live in your current country
  • a power of attorney, if someone applies for you

That guide also names a separate payment form (A1310). The fund's own page says the bilingual form already contains the declaration. Use the current forms in the fund's package and read their instructions.

Where to send it

The brochure says you can file the application, without formal requirements, with any German pension office, a German embassy or a German consulate. You may write in your own language. You need no agent, representative or interpreter (brochure, PDF page 12).

All About Berlin gives this postal address: Deutsche Rentenversicherung Bund, 10704 Berlin (guide). The brochure's imprint shows the same postal code for that office (brochure). The fund's form package page says to send the form to your pension insurance carrier. Check the form's instructions for the office that handles your case.

Do not send it before the 24 months are over. All About Berlin says the documents are returned if you do (guide).

How long it takes

All About Berlin's guide gives these times (guide):

  • 1 to 6 months from your application to a decision
  • about 6 more weeks to be paid after you confirm the refund letter
  • about 2 more months if the money goes to a bank account outside Germany

Germany Pension Refund reports that for its own managed claims the middle time from a complete application to the money reaching its escrow account was 40.5 days, and 229 of its latest 300 refunds arrived within 90 days. That is the company's own figure for its own cases (Germany Pension Refund).

Do it yourself or get help

Applying is free. The brochure says you do not need an agent (PDF page 12).

Some companies take over the paperwork for a share of the refund, paid only if the refund arrives:

  • Germany Pension Refund charges 9.75% of the refund, with a cap of €2,500 including VAT and no minimum (pricing page).
  • FundsBack lists a standard success fee of 9.9% with a minimum of €899 (FundsBack).

Tax in India
